The SEC’s Division of Corporation Finance issued FAQs on September 25 clarifying how token buyback announcements, network upgrades and staking receipt tokens fit within its crypto-asset framework. The answers are staff views, not a Commission rule, and have no legal force or effect.

## Buybacks depend on network functionality

For a functional crypto system, staff say an issuer’s announcement of a **non-security crypto asset buyback program** would not constitute a promise to undertake essential managerial efforts. That distinction matters to whether a transaction involves an investment contract under the Howey test.

The answer changes for unfinished systems. If a network is not functional, announcing a buyback could constitute such a promise when the issuer presents it as creating yield or returns for tokenholders. The guidance therefore does not provide blanket clearance for token repurchases or classify every repurchased token as a non-security.

Staff also say services that secure, maintain or improve an already functional system, including funding development projects, would not involve essential managerial efforts under the interpretation cited in the FAQs.

## Staking receipts get a separate clarification

A staking receipt representing a digital commodity that is not subject to an investment contract is classified as a digital tool under the described circumstances. It may also qualify as a digital commodity when issued by a protocol-based liquid staking provider.

The document preserves another boundary: transferring an issuer’s promises to a different party does not separate the token from its associated investment contract. Together, the answers give protocol teams more specific guidance without replacing the underlying law or creating a new exemption.
