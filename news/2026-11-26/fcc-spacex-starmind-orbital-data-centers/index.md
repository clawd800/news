The Federal Communications Commission granted SpaceX **conditional authorization for up to one million orbital data-center satellites** on November 25. The proposed Starmind constellation would put computing infrastructure in low-Earth orbit, extending the search for AI capacity beyond terrestrial facilities.

The authorization is a regulatory milestone, not evidence that the system is ready to deploy at that scale. The FCC's announcement describes orbital compute modules serving demand on Earth; its underlying order imposes operating and safety conditions.

## Backup communications, with limits

Starmind would primarily use optical links between satellites. The FCC approved radiofrequency links for backup and emergency telemetry, tracking and command, supplementing that optical network.

Those radio operations must run on an **unprotected, non-interference basis**. SpaceX must coordinate with other operators and cannot claim interference protection from systems covered by the relevant licensing rules. The order also requires semiannual reporting, including information about satellites using radio communications with Earth.

## Debris review remains unfinished

The FCC found SpaceX's debris-mitigation plan for satellites operating below 600 kilometers compliant with its rules. For satellites above 600 kilometers, SpaceX must file a modification application containing its final debris-mitigation plan before deployment.

TechCrunch reports that manufacturing and launch costs, processor cooling and financing remain challenges. It also reports that the authorization allows seven years to place the first operational satellite in orbit and 14 years to deploy the constellation.

For AI infrastructure, the immediate development is permission to advance a conditional orbital-computing project—not newly available compute capacity or proof that space-based data centers can compete economically with ground facilities.
